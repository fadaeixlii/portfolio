## The Problem

Real estate investors in the Netherlands were making decisions based on outdated quarterly reports and gut feeling. The publicly available data from the CBS and Kadaster was comprehensive but scattered across multiple APIs, published in different formats, and updated on different schedules. No single tool aggregated this data into actionable forecasts at the neighborhood level.

The existing tools that did attempt analytics were either too expensive for independent investors or too simplistic — showing city-level averages that masked the dramatic price differences between neighborhoods just a few kilometers apart.

## The Approach

The project required two distinct systems: a data pipeline that could ingest, normalize, and store Dutch housing data from multiple government sources, and a frontend that could visualize trends and present forecasts in a way that supported investment decisions.

I chose Python for the data pipeline because of its ecosystem around pandas and statistical modeling. The frontend and API were built in TypeScript with React and Node.js. PostgreSQL was the natural choice for time-series property data — its window functions and CTEs made the complex analytical queries performant without needing a specialized OLAP database.

The forecasting approach used a combination of historical price trends, demographic data, and infrastructure development plans. The model was deliberately conservative: rather than predicting exact prices, it produced confidence intervals and trend directions. Overconfident predictions in real estate are worse than no predictions at all.

## The Solution

The data pipeline ran on a weekly schedule, pulling from three primary sources: the Kadaster (land registry transactions), CBS StatLine (demographic and economic indicators), and municipal open data portals (zoning changes, infrastructure projects). Each source had its own ingestion adapter that handled authentication, pagination, and format differences.

Raw data was normalized into a common schema: location (postal code + neighborhood), timestamp, metric type, and value. This flat structure made it possible to query across sources without complex joins. A materialized view layer pre-computed the neighborhood-level aggregates that powered the frontend dashboards.

The frontend presented three views. The **map view** showed a choropleth of price trends by neighborhood, with color intensity indicating year-over-year change. The **comparison view** let users place two neighborhoods side-by-side across all available metrics. The **forecast view** displayed the statistical model's output: trend direction, confidence band, and the key factors driving the prediction.

Performance was critical — the map view rendered thousands of data points. I used server-side aggregation to reduce the payload, sending only the data the current viewport needed. The PostgreSQL queries were optimized with partial indexes on the most common filter combinations (city + metric type + time range).

## Impact

The platform indexed **120,000+ property transactions** across the Netherlands. Neighborhood-level forecasts achieved a **directional accuracy of 78%** over six-month periods — significantly better than the city-level averages investors were previously using. The average API response time stayed under **200ms** even for complex cross-neighborhood comparisons.

## Reflections

The most valuable technical decision was keeping the forecasting model simple. Early prototypes used a more complex ML approach that was marginally more accurate but impossible to explain to users. Switching to a transparent statistical model with visible factors (population growth, new transit lines, housing supply) made the forecasts trustworthy rather than just accurate. Users need to understand why a prediction was made before they will act on it.
